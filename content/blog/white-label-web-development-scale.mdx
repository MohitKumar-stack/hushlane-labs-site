White label web development is how an agency ships a site under its own name when the internal team is already booked. The client still sees one vendor. The repository, the domain, and the public credit stay with the agency or the client. Hushlane Labs does that work for agencies in the United States, the United Kingdom, Australia, and Canada. Delivery is based in India. The useful question is not whether a partner can “do websites.” It is whether the site can launch without introducing a second brand.

## White label web development starts from the sale you already made

Most briefs arrive as a proposal that has already been sold. Sometimes that proposal is a Figma file. Sometimes it is a sitemap and a sentence about what done means. White label web development here is implementation with judgment: service pages, landing pages, resource hubs, and a content model an editor can understand. If the job turns into a product — a configurator, a client library, a portal — that is named before the estimate and priced separately.

The first step is an NDA, then the brief. We sign before you send client names, credentials, or a deck. The brief is the proposal you already sold, plus a sentence on what done means. A mutual NDA also comes before customer records or anything that is not already public. That order is deliberate. A studio that wants the logo and the password on the same day has the sequence backwards.

## What the agency still owns

The hosting account, the analytics property, and the public credit stay with your agency or your client. The domain belongs to the agency or the client. Hushlane takes a credit only if you ask for one in writing. Case studies on this site are published only with permission.

That ownership is the difference between a quiet build and a subcontract that becomes visible. If a partner needs to publish from their own account “just for the beta,” that is the moment to stop. Moving accounts later is how credits leak. On day one, the repository and the hosting project should already be in the agency’s or the client’s name.

Acredge is the web example on this site. It is a real estate proptech platform, live at acredge.ai. The public problem it addresses is scattered listings, unverified information, and slow responses, while developers and agents lose leads to poor follow-up. The build includes smart property search and discovery with filters, AI-assisted recommendations and queries, and lead and listing management for agents and developers. The stack named on the case study is Next.js, React, and Google Cloud. The result line is that it is live at acredge.ai, built end to end. There is no traffic number, no conversion rate, and no claim about search rankings on that page, and this note will not invent one.

## What a normal website engagement includes

A website engagement is a page map, a responsive build, a CMS and an editor guide, a technical SEO foundation, and a launch pack. The page map is the list of templates and one-off pages required to launch, including what can wait. The build comes from your Figma, or from a restrained interface if you need the studio to design. Search is part of the build: each template gets one H1, a unique title, a meta description, internal links, and images with alt text. That is a foundation. It is not a promise of rankings.

The process on the service page is short on purpose. Read the sale. Lock the templates. Build in the open, with preview links your team can review. Hand over the launch. Reviews happen on a date. Feedback lives in one thread, so the work is not reconstructed from five inboxes. Your Slack or Teams is the channel. There is no contact with the end client unless you ask in writing.

## How this scales a team that should not grow yet

Hiring a full-time developer for a burst of website work is a slow way to cover a quarter that is already sold. A freelancer can cover a page. A white-label partner is for the stretch where several client sites are in flight and the agency still wants one throat to choke. The partner sits behind the agency. The client hired the agency, and the build still has to happen.

Scale, in this sense, is not a headcount chart. It is the ability to accept a website the team cannot start this month, without putting a stranger on the client call. The fit check comes first. You get a reply within 24 hours on business days. If the studio is the wrong partner, that reply says so. The first reply is a fit check, not a 40-page deck.

Dates are the other half of scale. On-time means the dates in the statement of work. If a date has to move, you hear it before that day, with what changed. A partner who agrees to every date in the proposal has not read it. Websites are a shorter list than apps. The timeline section of the working agreement says we would rather name a shorter list than miss a date that was never real.

## What to refuse before kickoff

White label web development fails in ordinary ways. The scope is a brochure in the proposal and a product in week three. The CMS is chosen because the partner already has an account. The analytics property is created under the partner’s email. The footer gains a second logo “for the portfolio.” None of those are technical mysteries. They are boundary failures.

Write the boundary down. If a date, a screen, or a permission matters, it goes in the statement of work. Memory is a bad scope tool. Ask whose name is on the accounts, whether the partner will contact your client, what they will refuse, who is actually in the codebase, and what happens to access on the last day. You should be able to remove the partner from GitHub, hosting, analytics, and chat in an afternoon. Secrets belong in a vault you own.

Also ask what “done” means for the editor. A CMS an editor cannot understand is not a handoff. The editor guide is part of the launch pack because the agency’s team, or the client’s team, has to change a headline after the studio is gone.

## Staffing without a rotating cast

You should know who is in the repository next month. A named lead is part of the working agreement. We do not send a rotating cast under one account manager. Australian reviews are booked on purpose. The rest of that collaboration is async and written, with a shared window for review. A weekly note is the default. Live calls happen when a review needs them. Your timezone sets the meeting.

That staffing rule matters more on websites than people expect, because websites attract drive-by edits. A rotating contractor will “just tweak the header” in a way the next person cannot explain. A named lead keeps the page map and the CMS model in one head.

## Handoff, or a retainer you chose earlier

The ending is chosen before kickoff, not on the last Friday. One ending is a clean handoff: repository, access, and notes. The other is a retainer. Both are decided before the work starts. The party named in the statement of work owns the code once the related invoices are paid. Repositories transfer at handoff. The studio does not keep a private fork to resell.

If the site needs care after launch, say so in the brief. Care is a different engagement from the launch. Pretending they are the same is how a fixed website becomes an unpaid inbox.

## A practical way to start

Send the proposal you already closed, the date the client expects, and whether the studio should stay off the call. Do not include passwords or customer records in the first email. The NDA comes before that material. If you would rather talk first, book the 30-minute call. The invite includes a Google Meet link. Either way, the reply is within 24 hours on business days.

White label web development is not a shortcut around scope. It is a way to build the site you already sold, under your name, with the accounts in the right place and a person you can name. The rest is a page map, a date, and a handoff your team can edit without calling us.
