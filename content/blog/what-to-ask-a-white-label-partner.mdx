A white-label partner is easy to praise in a proposal and awkward to explain after a client finds a second logo in the repository. Ask these before the statement of work is signed. The useful answers are specific. “We are fully white-label” is not an answer.

## Whose name is on the accounts?

The repository, the hosting project, the analytics property, and any app store listing should already be in the agency’s or the client’s name on day one. Moving them later is how credits leak. If a partner needs to publish from their own developer account “just for the beta,” that is the moment to stop.

## Will you contact our client?

The default should be no. Reviews happen with the agency. A partner joins an end-client call only when the agency invites them and stays in the thread. Put that in the statement of work, not in a slide.

## What will you refuse?

A serious studio can name work it will not automate and screens it will not pretend are in scope. Money movement, customer messages, and filings need a person in the loop until someone has measured the errors. If the partner agrees to every sentence in the proposal, they have not read it.

## Who is actually in the codebase?

Ask for the name of the lead who will still be there next month. Staffing marketplaces hide behind a rotating cast. You are putting your brand on this person’s pull requests.

## What happens to access on the last day?

You should be able to remove the partner from GitHub, hosting, analytics, and chat in an afternoon, and they should already have a checklist for it. Secrets belong in a vault you own. They do not belong in a personal password manager on another continent.

## How do you move a date?

On-time delivery is a date in the statement of work, plus a rule for what happens when the date is wrong. You want to hear about a slip before the day arrives, with the cause. A partner who only reports status when you ask is already late.

## Can we operate it without you?

Ask to see a runbook from a previous engagement, even a redacted one. If the only way to understand the system is a call, you do not own the work yet. Our [working agreement](/how-we-work) is written so this list is boring. That is the point.

## How to score the answers

Do not just collect answers. Score them. For each of the seven questions above, give the partner one point for an answer that is specific, one for an answer that is written down, and one for an answer you can check. A specific answer names a person, an account, or a date. A written answer appears in the statement of work or the working agreement, not only in a sales call. A checkable answer is something you can verify yourself, such as who owns the repository on day one.

A partner that scores well on all seven is rare. That is fine. What matters is the pattern. If every weak answer is on the same topic, such as access or contact with your client, that topic is where the engagement will go wrong. Ask the question again in writing. A partner who is comfortable with the answer will put it in writing the same day.

## Red flags that should end the conversation

Some answers are not a negotiation point. They are a reason to stop.

- The partner will publish a beta from their own developer account and move it later.
- The partner wants to join your client's calls without being asked.
- The partner asks you to email passwords or customer records before any agreement is signed.
- The partner agrees to every date in the proposal without reading the scope.
- The partner cannot name the person who will still be on your project next month.
- The partner treats your client's name as a logo for their own site.

Any one of these is a reason to look elsewhere. You are putting your brand on the work, so a partner who is careless with your accounts or your client is careless with your reputation.

## What to settle before the statement of work is signed

Most of the seven questions turn into lines in the statement of work. Settle these before anyone writes code:

1. A mutual NDA, signed before credentials, customer data, or any document that names the client.
2. The accounts that hold the repository, hosting project, analytics property, and any app store listing, in the agency's or the client's name from day one.
3. The rule for client contact. The default is no contact unless the agency asks in writing.
4. The named lead, and how you will be told if that changes.
5. The dates, and the rule for what happens when a date has to move.
6. Who owns the code. The party named in the statement of work should own it once the related invoices are paid, and the repository should transfer at handoff.
7. The handoff checklist: how access is removed from GitHub, hosting, analytics, and chat on the last day.

If a partner resists writing any of these down, you have learned something useful before it cost you anything.

## Frequently asked questions

### What should I ask a white-label partner before signing?

Ask whose name is on the accounts, whether they will contact your client, what they will refuse, who is in the codebase, what happens to access on the last day, how they move a date, and whether you can operate the work without them.

### Should the partner ever contact my client?

The default should be no. Reviews happen with the agency. A partner joins an end-client call only when the agency invites them and stays in the thread.

### Who should own the code and the accounts?

The accounts should be in the agency's or the client's name on day one. The party named in the statement of work should own the code once the related invoices are paid, and the repository should transfer at handoff.

### Is a rotating team a problem?

Yes. Ask for the name of the lead who will still be there next month. You are putting your brand on that person's pull requests, so a rotating cast is a risk to the work and to your name.

### How do I know a partner will tell me when a date slips?

Put the rule in the statement of work: you hear about a slip before the day arrives, with the cause. A partner who only reports status when you ask is already late.
